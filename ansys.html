Ansys puts its simulation suite behind the 2027 aircraft

Ansys has given QMUAS access to its academic research simulation tools for the 2026/27 season. The grant covers fluid dynamics, structural, thermal and materials software, backed by high-performance computing, and runs through to the end of March 2027.

What we have access to

The grant runs from April 2026 to the end of March 2027, with a second tranche added in August, and covers most of the physics a fixed-wing aircraft runs into:

  • Fluid dynamics: 35 concurrent tasks for airflow work
  • Structural and mechanical: 35 concurrent tasks for stress and deflection
  • High-performance computing: 64 cores, with a further 32 for explicit LS-DYNA work
  • Thermal: 10 tasks for heat behaviour inside enclosed bays
  • Electromagnetics and high frequency: 5 tasks each, for antenna and interference work
  • Granta materials selection: 5 seats of materials data
  • STK: 5 tasks for mission and trajectory modelling

Why that matters to a student team

A commercial seat of this software costs more than our entire season budget. Without it, a student team designs on hand calculations and spreadsheets, builds the aircraft, and finds out what was wrong at the airfield. The IMechE UAS Challenge gives you one fly-off a year, so a mistake you discover in June is a mistake you carry for twelve months.

Simulation moves that discovery forward. We can put a design through its loads before anyone cuts material, compare two layouts in an afternoon instead of building both, and turn up to the design reviews with evidence rather than estimates. That last part is not a side benefit: the competition marks the design and development specification and the flight readiness review largely on whether you can show your decisions were reasoned.

The compute allocation matters as much as the licences do. A case that would tie up a laptop overnight finishes over lunch on 32 cores, and that is the difference between testing one idea a week and testing several.

Where we're using it

Airflow over the airframe; structural loads through the joints and mounts that carry the wing and the motor; thermal behaviour in the enclosed electronics bays; and materials selection when we are choosing between composite layups and printed parts.

We are keeping the results to ourselves until after the competition, since every team in the challenge is solving the same problem. But the process is the point, and it is one we could not run at all without this support.

What's next

The concept paper goes in this month, followed by the preliminary and critical design reviews over the winter, the design and development specification in March 2027 and the flight readiness review in June, before the fly-off at the end of June.
